# Week-7 campaign report: first evidence

*Benchmark campaign, week 7 of 12. Campaign `20261104-014744-standard`, run on machine A: a laptop with an AMD Ryzen 7 6800HS (8 cores / 16 threads), 23 GB of memory, Windows 11, on AC power.*

## Summary

The pilot campaign measured 29 configurations of the four workloads, 10 times each after 2 discarded warm-ups: 348 isolated runs in 18 minutes. Energy was read from the processor's RAPL counters through the Windows Energy Meter Interface, and every one of the 290 measured runs reproduced the reference result. For the same result, implementation choices changed the energy spent by up to **24×**. Most savings also made runs faster, with one exception: adding threads cut latency but raised energy.

## Key findings

1. **Storage format is the cheapest win.** Reading the join inputs from Parquet + Snappy instead of CSV cuts dynamic energy by **56 %** (95 % CI 46–59 %) and time by 55 %. JSON lines costs 3.6 times the energy of CSV (+262 %). Compression did not pay here: Zstandard made CSV 11 % more expensive, and Parquet + Zstd saved less (−41 %) than Parquet + Snappy (−56 %).
2. **Fastest is not greenest when threads are added.** For the join, the single-threaded hash join is the most frugal configuration. Every multi-threaded variant uses significantly more energy (+28 % to +44 % for the hash join, large effects), even though 4 threads cut latency by 18 % and sort-merge on 8 threads is the fastest configuration overall (−23 %).
3. **Indexes pay back quickly, if they fit the queries.** The inverted index repays its build energy after about **3 queries** and uses 96 % less energy than regular-expression scanning at 100 queries (2.3 J instead of 55.7 J). The B+-tree repays its build after about 150 queries and cuts energy by 81 % at 1,000 queries. A hash index cannot answer range queries, so at 100,000 queries it uses 6.9 times the energy of the B+-tree (+593 %).
4. **Incremental beats approximate.** Evaluating sliding windows incrementally cuts energy by **80 %** versus recomputing every window, with an exact result. The approximate variants did not improve on that: HyperLogLog used 2.7 times the energy of the exact incremental method (1.9 % error on distinct users), and a 10 % user sample used as much energy while erring by 15 %.
5. **Hardware counters matter for parallelism decisions.** A CodeCarbon-style software model (TDP share × CPU time) tracks the hardware closely overall (Spearman ρ = 0.91, Pearson r = 0.996), but it reverses **4 of the 45** verdicts the hardware makes with significance, all of them about thread counts. It would rank single-threaded sort-merge as greener than 8 threads, while the counter shows 8 threads using 33 % less energy (p = 0.004). A core boosting alone to its top frequency is inefficient, and a model that charges every CPU-second the same cannot see that.

## Quality of the measurements

* 290 of 290 measured runs valid, no errors; idle package power 6.17 W, drifting by 0.36 W over the campaign.
* 50 runs (17 %) had more background activity than the idle baseline plus one core. They are kept and reported, not silently removed.
* The median coefficient of variation of dynamic energy is 24 %: most runs last 0.25–3 s, and subtracting idle power amplifies relative noise. Some intervals are therefore wide (for example +6 % to +47 %), but every headline effect above is large and significant after Holm correction.

## Next steps (weeks 8–9)

* **Full campaign:** 30 repetitions per configuration on a quiet machine, to narrow the intervals.
* **Second machine:** Linux with Intel RAPL (measured rather than modelled on recent Intel parts), run from the pinned Docker image, to see which conclusions are hardware-specific.
* **Data scale:** repeat the join and indexing studies at small and larger scales; the parallelism penalty may shrink as inputs grow.
* **Cross-check:** an external wattmeter on one configuration, if one is available at SUP'COM.

## Limitations

- One machine, one operating system: results describe this laptop, not hardware in general.
- The package domain covers the CPU package only (cores, caches, memory controller, integrated GPU): DRAM, storage and the rest of the platform are not measured.
- On AMD processors RAPL energy is produced by an on-chip power model rather than a physical sensor (Schöne et al., 2021); differences between variants are trustworthy, absolute joules less so.
- Dynamic energy subtracts a single idle baseline; background activity that differs from idle is tracked per run and flagged.
- Windows CPU-time counters advance in 15.6 ms ticks, so the software model is coarse for very short runs.
